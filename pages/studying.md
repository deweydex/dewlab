# Studying here

This page is about two things: which course is yours, and how to get the
most from a page once you are in it.

## Choosing a course

If you are in a class, **your teacher will tell you which course your class
follows**. The courses match the QQI modules your college enrols you in, so
start with that one. The rest of this section is for anyone choosing on their
own, or curious about the others.

**Programming and Maths, Integrated** teaches two modules as one subject:
Programming and Design Principles, and Mathematics for Information
Technology. You write Python from the first page, and use it to explore
numbers, data, chance, graphs and change. It ends with a project you build in
a team. If you are new to both, start here.

**The Dewey Track** teaches the same two modules in a different way. It is
for people who found maths or programming hard before and want another way
in. Its pages ask you to guess first: you say what you think will happen, run
the code, and find out. Along the way you build a toolkit of your own
functions, and later pages use them.

The two courses cover the same ground, so a class takes one of them, not
both. Your teacher decides which.

**Programming and Design Principles** is the programming half of the
integrated course on its own, for a class taking only that module.

**Computational Methods** uses Python on bigger problems: matrices and the
pictures they move, text generation, simulations, algorithms, and finding
what is wrong with a program. It starts with the Python you need.

**Fundamentals of Object Oriented Programming** is for when you can write a
function and want to build larger programs out of classes of your own.

**Database Methods**, **Web Authoring** and **Full Stack** teach SQL, web
pages, and the two together.

## How to study here

**Guess before you run.** When a page asks what a cell will print, say what
you think first: out loud, in a comment, or in the box above the cell. Then
run it. A guess that turns out different is the most useful thing on the
page, because it shows you exactly which idea to look at again.

**Come back after a gap.** Something you met last week and use again today
stays with you longer than something you read twice in one evening. Going
back to an earlier page is a good use of time, not a step backwards.

**Try the "from earlier" problems.** Where a practice page has problems
marked "from earlier", they come from pages you did some time ago. They are
there on purpose, for the same reason.

**When you are stuck,** try these, in this order:

1. The hint under the cell, if there is one. The first hint asks you a
   question about what you can see.
2. **I'm not sure yet**, where a page asks for a guess. It opens the first
   hint, and you can still run the cell and see.
3. An earlier page. The topic tree shows what a page expects you to have
   met already.
4. The **Reference** panel, for a word or a function you do not remember.
5. A classmate, or your tutor. Show them the cell and what it printed.

**Mistakes are part of the work.** Nothing on this site marks you right or
wrong, and nothing is scored or sent anywhere. Where a page shows your
result beside another one, the difference is something to look at, not a
mark against you.
