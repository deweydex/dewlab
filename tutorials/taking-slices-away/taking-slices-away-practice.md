## 5. A half take away a quarter

What is $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Halves and quarters are different sizes.
2. Cut every half into 2 smaller slices. How many quarters is one half?
3. Now both are in quarters. Take the tops away.

**Think about:** a picture of a pizza cut into 4, with 2 slices shaded.
Now remove one slice.

**Try this next:** what is 1/2 − 1/8?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

One half is $\frac{2}{4}$. So

$$\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{2}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$$

</details>

## 6. Find a shared bottom number

What is $\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Thirds and halves are different sizes. Find a slice size that works
   for both.
2. 6 slices can be put in 3 equal groups, and in 2 equal groups.
3. Name both fractions in sixths. Then take the tops away.

**Think about:** is 2/3 more than one half? Then the answer must be
more than 0.

**Try this next:** what is 3/4 − 2/3?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

A common denominator is 6. Two thirds is $\frac{4}{6}$, and one half is
$\frac{3}{6}$. So

$$\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{4}{6} - \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{6}$$

</details>

## 7. What went differently here?

Somebody wrote this:

$$\frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3 - 1}{4 - 2} = \frac{2}{2}$$

How much pizza is $\frac{2}{2}$? Does that make sense, when we started
with $\frac{3}{4}$ and took some away? What would you do in their place?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. 2/2 is every slice of a pizza cut into 2. How much pizza is that?
2. Is that more or less than 3/4?
3. Taking away should leave less than we started with.

**Think about:** the slices are quarters and halves. What size were
they after the bottoms were taken away?

**Try this next:** find 3/4 − 1/2 in quarters.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one answer</summary>

$\frac{2}{2}$ is one whole pizza. That is more than the $\frac{3}{4}$
we started with, so it cannot be what is left. They took away the tops
*and* the bottoms. But the bottoms say the size of the slices, and the
slices are different sizes. In quarters, one half is $\frac{2}{4}$. So
$\frac{3}{4} - \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$. Here is one answer. Yours
may be different and work too.

</details>

## 8. Two paths, one answer

Start with one whole pizza. Take away a third. Then take away another
third. How much is left? Try two different ways, and see if they agree.

- **One at a time.** Write the whole pizza as 3/3. Take away 1/3, then
  1/3 again.
- **Add first.** Add the two thirds together. Then take that away from
  1.

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. One whole pizza is 3 thirds.
2. Take one third away. How many thirds are left? Then take another.
3. For the second path, what is 1/3 + 1/3?

**Think about:** why the two paths must give the same amount of pizza.

**Try this next:** 1 − 1/4 − 1/4, both ways.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

One at a time:
$\frac{3}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$, and then
$\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$.

Add first: $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$, and
$1 - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{3}{3} - \frac{2}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$.

The two paths agree: one third is left.

</details>

## 9. Check it by adding

Somebody found that $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{7}{12}$. You do
not need to take away to check it. Add the answer and the part taken
away: $\frac{7}{12} + \frac{1}{4}$. Do you get $\frac{5}{6}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Name 1/4 in twelfths.
2. Add it to 7/12.
3. Name 5/6 in twelfths, and compare.

**Think about:** why adding checks a take-away. Adding returns the
slices that were taken.

**Try this next:** check 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6 by adding.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

One quarter is $\frac{3}{12}$. So
$\frac{7}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{10}{12}$. And $\frac{5}{6}$ is
$\frac{10}{12}$ too. So the answer $\frac{7}{12}$ checks. Adding the
quarter again gives the start.

</details>

## 11. A stretch: taking away too much

What is $\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2}$? Here we take away more pizza than
we have. Make a guess, then run the cell.

```python exec
id: taking-away-too-much-1
from fractions import Fraction

print(Fraction(1, 4) - Fraction(1, 2))
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Name both in quarters. One half is 2 quarters.
2. So we have 1 quarter, and we take away 2 quarters.
3. After we take away the 1 quarter we have, how many more quarters do
   we still need to take?

**Think about:** a number line that continues past 0, to the left.

**Try this next:** what is 1/3 − 2/3?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

In quarters, it is $\frac{1}{4} - \frac{2}{4}$. We have 1 slice and
take away 2. The first slice takes us to 0. One more quarter still
needs to go. So the answer is one quarter *below* 0. We write it
$-\frac{1}{4}$, and Python prints `-1/4`.

Numbers below 0 are called *negative* numbers. On a number line, they
sit to the left of 0. Pizza cannot be negative. But money can: if you
have 1 euro and spend 2, you owe 1 euro. That means you must still
pay 1 euro.

</details>

## 12. From earlier: two slices together

From *Adding fractions: putting slices together*. What is
$\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Sixths work for both.
2. How many sixths is one third?
3. Add the tops. Can your answer have a shorter name?

**Think about:** which landmark your answer is.

**Try this next:** 1/4 + 1/8.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

One third is $\frac{2}{6}$. So
$\frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6}$, and that is $\frac{1}{2}$.

</details>

## 13. From earlier: which is bigger?

From *Comparing fractions: which is bigger?* Which is more pizza,
$\frac{5}{6}$ or $\frac{7}{8}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Each one is one slice less than a whole pizza.
2. What is $1 - \frac{5}{6}$? What is $1 - \frac{7}{8}$?
3. Which missing slice is smaller?

**Think about:** a smaller missing slice means closer to 1.

**Try this next:** which is bigger, 9/10 or 10/11?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

$\frac{5}{6}$ has $\frac{1}{6}$ missing. $\frac{7}{8}$ has
$\frac{1}{8}$ missing. An eighth is a thinner slice than a sixth. So
$\frac{7}{8}$ is closer to 1, and it is bigger.

</details>
