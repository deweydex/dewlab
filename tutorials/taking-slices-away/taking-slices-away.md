# Subtracting fractions: taking slices away

A pizza was cut into 8 equal slices. Now 5 slices are left on the
plate. A friend eats 2 of them.

<img src="take-away-same.svg" alt="Three pizzas of the same size, each cut into 8 equal slices. A minus sign is between the first two, and an equals sign is before the third. The first pizza has 5 slices shaded, the second has 2, and the third has 3.">

```question
id: five-slices-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

How much of the pizza is left now?

- 3/8
  - 5 slices take away 2 slices leaves 3 slices. Each slice is still
    one eighth.
- 3/0
  - That takes away the bottoms too: 8 − 8 = 0. But the slices are
    still eighths.
- 3
  - 3 slices are left. 3 slices is less than one whole pizza.
```

## The same size of slice

We write it with a minus sign, −. *Minus* means "take away".
*Subtracting* is another word for taking away.

$$\frac{5}{8} - \frac{2}{8} = \frac{3}{8}$$

The top numbers are taken away: 5 slices take away 2 slices is 3
slices. The bottom number stays 8, as it did for adding. We did not cut
the pizza again, so the slices are still eighths.

```question
id: the-same-size-of-slice-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

7/10 − 4/10 = {3/10|3/0|11/10}

9/12 − 9/12 = {0|1|9/12}
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. The bottoms are the same, so the slices are the same size.
2. Take the second top away from the first top.
3. For 9/12 − 9/12, we have 9 slices and we take away all 9. How many
   are left?

**Think about:** the bottom number says the size of each slice. Does
eating a slice change the size of the others?

**Try this next:** 11/12 − 1/12.

</details>

## Taking from a whole pizza

Now we start with one whole pizza, and take away one quarter.

<img src="whole-minus-quarter.svg" alt="Three pizzas of the same size, each cut into 4 equal slices. A minus sign is between the first two, and an equals sign is before the third. The first pizza has all 4 slices shaded, the second has 1, and the third has 3.">

Here, 1 has no bottom number. But the first page showed that one whole
pizza is every slice. A pizza cut into 4 slices is 4/4. So

$$1 = \frac{4}{4}$$

```question
id: taking-from-a-whole-pizza-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

What is 1 − 1/4?

- 3/4
  - One whole pizza is 4 quarters. Take one away, and 3 are left.
- 0
  - 1 take away 1 is 0. But we took away one slice, not the whole
    pizza.
- 1/4
  - 1/4 is the slice we took away. The question asks what is left.
```

## Slices of different sizes

Now $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}$. We have half a pizza. We take away a
third of a pizza. A half and a third are different sizes. So, as with
adding, we cannot count them together.

On the page
[Adding fractions: putting slices together](tutorial:adding-slices), we
cut both pizzas into sixths. We can do the same here.

```question
id: slices-of-different-sizes-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

One half is the same amount as {3/6|2/6|1/6}.

One third is the same amount as {2/6|3/6|1/6}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Cut every half into 3 smaller slices. How many slices is the whole
   pizza now? How many are in one half?
2. Cut every third into 2 smaller slices. How many slices is the whole
   pizza now? How many are in one third?
3. The fraction walls on the adding page show the same thing.

**Think about:** both fractions now have 6 on the bottom. That is a
common denominator.

**Try this next:** how many sixths is two thirds?

</details>

Now every slice is a sixth. So we can take slices away.

<img src="half-minus-third.svg" alt="Three pizzas of the same size, each cut into 6 equal slices. A minus sign is between the first two, and an equals sign is before the third. The first pizza has 3 slices shaded, the second has 2, and the third has 1.">

```question
id: slices-of-different-sizes-2
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

What is 1/2 − 1/3?

- 1/6
  - 3 sixths take away 2 sixths leaves 1 sixth.
- 0
  - The tops are both 1, and 1 − 1 = 0. But a half is bigger than a
    third, so some pizza is left.
- 5/6
  - 1/2 + 1/3 is 5/6. This time we take away.
```

```question
id: slices-of-different-sizes-3
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

What is 3/4 − 1/3? Twelfths work for both.

- 5/12
  - 3/4 is 9/12, and 1/3 is 4/12. 9 twelfths take away 4 twelfths
    leaves 5 twelfths.
- 2/1
  - That takes away the tops, 3 − 1, and the bottoms, 4 − 3. But 2/1
    is two whole pizzas, more than we started with.
- 9/12
  - 3/4 is 9/12. The third still needs to be taken away.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. A pizza cut into 12 slices can be put in 4 equal groups. Each
   group is one quarter. How many slices is 3 quarters?
2. The same 12 slices can be put in 3 equal groups. How many
   slices is one third?
3. Now both are in twelfths. Take the tops away.

**Think about:** the answer must be less than 3/4, because we took
something away.

**Try this next:** what is 2/3 − 1/4?

</details>

## A machine that checks

`Fraction` can take away too. In Python, the minus sign is `-`. The
first line, `from fractions import Fraction`, gets the tool ready, as
before.

```python exec
id: a-machine-that-checks-1
from fractions import Fraction

print(Fraction(1, 2) - Fraction(1, 3))
```

- 0
  - The tops are both 1, and 1 − 1 is 0.
- 5/6

## Your rule, in your words

Before you read our version, say how to take one fraction away from
another, in your own words. Write it in the Notes panel or on paper, or
say it aloud.

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way to say it</summary>

If the slices are the same size, take the top numbers away. The bottom
number stays the same, because the slices keep their size. If the
slices are different sizes, cut them again until they are the same
size, as for adding. Then take the tops away. To take a fraction away
from 1, write 1 with the same bottom number first: 1 is 4/4, or 10/10.
Your way of saying it may be clearer than ours.

</details>

## Looking back

To find 1 − 1/4, we wrote 1 as 4/4. Why were we allowed to do that?
Which earlier page showed it?

A challenge: start with one whole pizza. Take away half a pizza. Then
take away a quarter of a pizza, then an eighth, and so on. Each slice
is half of the one before. The program below does five steps. How much
pizza is left after each step? Compare it with the challenge on the
adding page. What do you notice?

```python challenge
# One whole pizza. Take away a half, then a quarter, then an eighth.
from fractions import Fraction

left = Fraction(1)
for step in range(1, 6):
    left = left - Fraction(1, 2 ** step)
    print(step, left)
```
