## Further right, bigger

Each step to the right adds 1. So the numbers get bigger as we move to
the right.

```question
id: further-right-bigger-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

5 is bigger than 2. Where is 5 on the line?

- To the right of 2
  - From 2, three more steps to the right land on 5.
- To the left of 2
  - To the left of 2 are 1 and 0. Those are smaller than 2.
- In the same place as 2
  - Each whole number has its own tick. 2 and 5 have different ticks.
```

## Between the whole numbers

On the page
[Comparing fractions: which is bigger?](tutorial:which-is-bigger), we
used a line from 0 to 1. One half was halfway along it. That line is a
small piece of this line. It fits between the ticks for 0 and 1.

```question
id: between-the-whole-numbers-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

One half sits between
{0 and 1|1 and 2|4 and 5}.
```

Every fraction has a place on the line. Some fractions are more than
1, so their place is further to the right.

```question
id: between-the-whole-numbers-2
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Where does 7/2 go on the line?

- Halfway between 3 and 4
  - 7/2 is 7 halves. 6 halves make 3 wholes, and one more half makes
    3 and a half.
- On the tick for 7
  - The top number is 7. The bottom number, 2, says each piece is a
    half, not a whole.
- Between 0 and 1
  - Many fractions are less than 1. This one has more halves than one
    whole needs.
```

**Think about:** where 3 and one half sits, between two ticks.

## Below zero

Now think about a thermometer. A thermometer shows how hot or cold it
is. Its scale is a number line, standing up. Water freezes at 0
degrees. On a cold winter night, the temperature can fall below 0.

```question

{1 degree below zero|1 degree above zero|5 degrees}.
```

## A name for below zero

A number below zero is called a *negative number*{.term}. We write it with a
short line in front: −3. We say "minus three". On the number line, the
negative numbers are to the left of 0. Look at the first picture
again.

```question
id: a-name-for-below-zero-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

−3 is
{3|0|−6}
steps from 0.

It is on the
{left|right}
of 0.
```

3 is also 3 steps from 0, on the right. So −3 and 3 are the same
distance from 0, on different sides. We call 3 the *opposite* of −3.
Every number on the right of 0 has an opposite on the left, as far from
0 as it is. (This is a different idea from the partner of a fraction on
[Dividing fractions: how many fit?](tutorial:how-many-fit). That partner
is the fraction turned over.)

## Which is bigger, −1 or −3?

On the right of 0, a number further to the right is bigger. Is that
true on the left of 0 too?

```question
id: which-is-bigger-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Which number is bigger, −1 or −3?

- −1
  - −1 is to the right of −3. On a thermometer, −1 degrees is warmer.
- −3
  - 3 is bigger than 1, so −3 can feel bigger. Look where each one
    sits on the line.
- They are the same size
  - Both are below zero. They sit at different places on the line.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Find −1 and −3 on the first picture.
2. Which one is further to the right?
3. Now think of two cold nights. One night is −1 degrees: 1 degree
   below zero. The other night is −3 degrees: 3 degrees below zero.
   Which night is colder?

**Think about:** a colder night has a smaller temperature.

**Try this next:** which is bigger, −2 or −5?

</details>

A number further to the right is always bigger. This is true on both
sides of 0. So −1 is bigger than −3. And every negative number is
smaller than 0.

{smaller than|bigger than|the same as}

## Moving along the line

Adding 3 moves 3 steps to the right. Start at 2 and add 3: you land on
5. So 2 + 3 = 5.

Taking away 3 moves 3 steps to the left.

```question
id: moving-along-the-line-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

Start at 2, and take 3 steps to the left. 2 − 3 lands on
{−1|1|5}.

Start at −4, and take 3 steps to the right. −4 + 3 lands on
{−1|−7|1}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Put your finger on 2 in the first picture.
2. Move one tick to the left, and say where you are: 1.
3. Move two more ticks, one at a time. Where does the third step land?

**Think about:** a step from 0 to the left lands on −1.

**Try this next:** start at 1 and take 4 steps to the left.

</details>

## A checking machine

Python can check moves along the line. Python is a language for
computers. It writes minus with a short line, `-`.

```python exec
id: a-checking-machine-1
print(2 + 3)
print(-4 + 3)
print(1 - 4)
```

```predict
type: number

Before you run it: what will the last line, `1 - 4`, print? Start at 1
and take 4 steps to the left.
```

The first line prints 5, and the second prints −1, as the steps
showed. Change the numbers and run it again. Try a move that crosses 0
from the left, like `-2 + 5`.

## A line you can change

The program below draws a number line with some dots on it. The list
`places` says where the dots go. Python writes one half as `0.5`, and
one and a half as `1.5`.

```python exec
id: a-line-you-can-change-1
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

places = [-4, 1.5, -1, 3]

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 1))
plt.plot(places, [0] * len(places), "o", markersize=10, clip_on=False)
plt.xticks(range(-5, 6))
plt.yticks([])
plt.xlim(-5, 5)
plt.ylim(0, 1)
```

```predict
type: choice

Before you run it: which dot will be furthest to the left?

- The dot for −4
  - −4 is 4 steps to the left of 0. The others are closer.
- The dot for −1
  - 1 is a smaller number than 4, so −1 can feel further left.
- The dot for 1.5
  - 1.5 is the only number here that is not whole. It is still to the
    right of 0.
```

The first line, `import`, gets the drawing tools ready. You do not need
to change it. Change the numbers in `places`, and run it again.

- Can you put a dot halfway between −1 and 0?
- Can you put a dot on 0, and a dot on each side of it, as far away?
- Can you put four dots in a row, one step apart, all below zero?

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>What each line does</summary>

- `places = [...]` is the list of places for the dots.
- `plt.figure(...)` makes a picture that is wide and not very tall.
- `plt.plot(...)` puts a round dot at each place, on the line.
- `plt.xticks(range(-5, 6))` puts a tick on each whole number from −5
  to 5.
- `plt.yticks([])` hides the numbers on the side. We do not need
  them.
- `plt.xlim(-5, 5)` shows the line from −5 to 5 only.
- `plt.ylim(0, 1)` puts the dots on the bottom edge, where the numbers
  are.

</details>

## How far apart?

How far is it from 2 to 5? Count the steps: 3, 4, 5. That is 3 steps.
The number of steps between two places is the *distance* between them.

```question
id: how-far-apart-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

From −1 to 3 is
{4|2|3}
steps.

From −4 to −1 is
{3|5|−3}
steps.
```

2. Count the steps to 0. Then count the steps from 0 to 3.
3. Add the two counts together.

**Think about:** a distance counts steps. It is never below zero.

**Try this next:** how far is it from −2 to 2?

</details>

## Your rule, in your words

Before you read our version, say in your own words where a negative
number goes on the line. Then say how to tell which of two numbers is
bigger. Write it in the Notes panel or on paper, or say it aloud.

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way to say it</summary>

Every number has a place on one line. The whole numbers have ticks, the
same distance apart. Fractions sit between the ticks. A negative number
sits to the left of 0, as far from 0 as its opposite on the right. A
number further to the right is bigger, on both sides of 0. Adding moves
to the right, and taking away moves to the left. Your way of saying it
may be clearer than ours.

</details>

## Looking back

Somebody says that −10 is bigger than −2, because 10 is bigger than 2.
What picture would you show them?

A challenge: the program below starts at 2. Then it makes each move in
the list `moves`, and prints where it lands. A move of `3` is 3 steps
to the right, and a move of `-5` is 5 steps to the left. Can you choose
moves that land on −4? Can you choose three moves that end where they
started, at 2? What do you notice about those three moves?

```python challenge
# Start at 2, then make each move in turn.
place = 2
moves = [3, -5]
for move in moves:
    place = place + move
    print(place)
```
