# Adding fractions: putting slices together

Two friends share one pizza, cut into 8 equal slices. One friend eats 3
slices. The other friend eats 2 slices.

<img src="same-size-sum.svg" alt="Three pizzas of the same size, each cut into 8 equal slices. A plus sign is between the first two, and an equals sign is before the third. The first pizza has 3 slices shaded, the second has 2, and the third has 5.">

```question
id: two-friends-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

How much of the pizza did the two friends eat in total?

- 5/8
  - 3 slices and 2 slices make 5 slices. Each slice is still one eighth.
- 5/16
  - Adding the tops and adding the bottoms feels natural. But the pizza
    still has 8 slices, not 16.
- 5
  - The friends ate 5 slices. 5 slices is less than one whole pizza.
```

## The same size of slice

We write it like this:

$$\frac{3}{8} + \frac{2}{8} = \frac{5}{8}$$

The top numbers are added: 3 slices and 2 slices make 5 slices. The
bottom number stays 8. The slices are still eighths, because the pizza
is still cut into 8.

On the practice page for *Fractions: one whole pizza, many slices*,
problem 6 added seven slices of $\frac{1}{7}$. One choice there was
$\frac{7}{49}$. That choice adds the tops *and* the bottoms. Many people
guess this. But the bottom number says the size of each slice. Adding
more slices does not change their size.

```question
id: the-same-size-of-slice-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

2/5 + 2/5 = {4/5|4/10|2/5}

3/10 + 4/10 + 2/10 = {9/10|9/30|9}
```

```question
id: the-same-size-of-slice-2
type: fill-in-the-blank

5/8 + 5/8 = {10/8|10/16|5/8}

10/8 is {more than|less than|exactly} one whole pizza.
```

2. One pizza has only 8 slices.
3. So where do the other slices come from?

**Think about:** 8/8 is one whole pizza. How many slices are left after
that?

</details>

## Slices of different sizes

Now a harder sum. One pizza is cut into 2 slices. Another pizza of the
same size is cut into 3 slices. We have one slice of each.

<img src="half-and-third.svg" alt="Two pizzas of the same size, with a plus sign between them. The first is cut into 2 equal slices, with 1 shaded. The second is cut into 3 equal slices, with 1 shaded.">

That is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}$. Somebody adds the tops and adds the
bottoms. They write $\frac{2}{5}$.

```question
id: slices-of-different-sizes-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

2/5 is {less than|more than|the same as} 1/2.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Picture a pizza cut into 5 slices. Half of 5 slices is two and a
   half slices.
2. 2/5 has 2 slices. Is that more than two and a half, or less?
3. Picture the line with 0, one half and 1 on it, from the page
   *Comparing fractions: which is bigger?*

**Think about:** we started with half a pizza, and then added more
pizza.

**Try this next:** is 2/5 more than 1/3, or less?

</details>

So 2/5 cannot be the answer. We started with half a pizza, and then we
added more. The answer must be more than one half.

A half is a big slice. A third is a smaller slice. We cannot count
them together, because the slices are different sizes. One big slice and one small slice are not "2" of
anything.

## Cutting both into sixths

Here is an idea. Cut both pizzas again, so that all the slices are the
same size. Sixths work for both. Here is the half, with sixths under
it.

<img src="half-in-sixths.svg" alt="A fraction wall of three bars of the same length. The top bar is one whole. The next bar is cut into 2 halves, with 1 shaded. The bottom bar is cut into 6 sixths, with 3 shaded. The shaded parts of the two bars end at the same place.">

And here is the third, with sixths under it.

<img src="third-in-sixths.svg" alt="A fraction wall of three bars of the same length. The top bar is one whole. The next bar is cut into 3 thirds, with 1 shaded. The bottom bar is cut into 6 sixths, with 2 shaded. The shaded parts of the two bars end at the same place.">

```question
id: cutting-both-into-sixths-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

One half is the same amount as {3|2|6} sixths.

One third is the same amount as {2|3|1} sixths.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Look at the first wall. Put your finger on the shaded half.
2. Move your finger down to the sixths. Count the shaded sixths.
3. Do the same with the second wall.

**Think about:** the page on equivalent fractions. Cutting every slice
again gives a new name for the same amount.

**Try this next:** how many sixths is two thirds?

</details>

Now every slice is a sixth. So we can count them.

<img src="into-sixths.svg" alt="Three pizzas of the same size, each cut into 6 equal slices. A plus sign is between the first two, and an equals sign is before the third. The first pizza has 3 slices shaded, the second has 2, and the third has 5.">

```question
id: cutting-both-into-sixths-2
type: fill-in-the-blank

3/6 + 2/6 = {5/6|5/12|1/6}

So 1/2 + 1/3 = {5/6|2/5|2/6}
```

5/6 is more than one half. That fits with what we found before: we
started with half a pizza, and added more.

```question
id: cutting-both-into-sixths-3
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Somebody cuts both pizzas into twelfths, not sixths. One half is 6/12.
One third is 4/12. What will they get?

- 10/12, the same amount as 5/6
  - Twelfths are sixths, cut again. Cutting every slice again does not
    change the amount.
- 10/12, which is more than 5/6
  - 10 is a bigger number than 5. But each twelfth is half the size of
    a sixth.
- 10/24
  - That adds the bottoms too, 12 and 12. The slices are still
    twelfths.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Add the twelfths: 6 twelfths and 4 twelfths.
2. Now cut every sixth in 5/6 into 2 smaller slices. How many twelfths
   is that?
3. Compare the two answers.

**Think about:** does it matter which slice size we choose, if it works
for both fractions?

</details>

## A name for what we did

Let's look at what we did. We could not add a half and a third. So we
cut both pizzas again, until all the slices were the same size. Then we
added the slices.

Now we can give the parts of a fraction their names.

- The bottom number is called the *denominator*. It says how many
  slices the whole was cut into.
- The top number is called the *numerator*. It says how many of those
  slices we have.

For 1/2 + 1/3, we found a bottom number that both fractions could
have: 6. A bottom number that two fractions share is called a *common
denominator*. *Common* here means shared by both.

```question
id: a-name-for-what-we-did-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

For 1/3 + 1/4, a common denominator is {12|7|4}.

1/3 is the same amount as {4/12|3/12|1/12}.

1/4 is the same amount as {3/12|4/12|1/12}.

So 1/3 + 1/4 = {7/12|2/7|2/12}
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. A pizza cut into 12 slices can be put in 3 equal groups. Each
   group is one third. How many slices is that?
2. The same 12 slices can be put in 4 equal groups. Each group is
   one quarter.
3. Could 7 slices be put in 3 equal groups?

**Think about:** what 3 × 4 is, and why it works for thirds and for
quarters.

**Try this next:** find 1/2 + 1/5. Try tenths.

</details>

## A machine that checks

On the page about equivalent fractions, we met the Python tool
`Fraction`. It can add fractions too. The first line,
`from fractions import Fraction`, gets the tool ready.

```python exec
id: a-machine-that-checks-1
from fractions import Fraction

print(Fraction(1, 2) + Fraction(1, 3))
```

```predict

- 2/5
  - Add the tops, and add the bottoms.
- 2/6
  - Two slices, and sixths as the size of each slice.
```

Python printed 5/6, the same as the pizzas. Now try your own sums.

## Your rule, in your words

Before you read our version, say how to add two fractions, in your own
words. Write it in the Notes panel or on paper, or say it aloud.

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way to say it</summary>

If the slices are the same size, add the top numbers. The bottom number
stays the same, because the slices do not change size. If the slices
are different sizes, cut them again until they are the same size. That
new bottom number is a common denominator. Then add the top numbers.
Your way of saying it may be clearer than ours.

</details>

## Looking back

For 1/2 + 1/3, we cut both pizzas into sixths. For 3/8 + 2/8, we did
not cut anything. Why was the second sum different?

A challenge: take half a pizza. Then add a quarter, then an eighth,
then a sixteenth. Each new slice is half of the one before. The program
below adds the first five slices. `2 ** step` is 2, then 4, then 8, and
so on, from the page on powers. How much pizza is missing after each
step? Can you make it run 20 steps? Does the total ever reach 1?

```python challenge
# Half a pizza, then a quarter, then an eighth, and so on.
from fractions import Fraction

total = Fraction(0)
for step in range(1, 6):
    total = total + Fraction(1, 2 ** step)
    print(step, total)
```
