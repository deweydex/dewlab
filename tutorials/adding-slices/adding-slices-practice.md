## 3. Continue the pattern

Start with $\frac{1}{6}$ of a pizza. Add $\frac{1}{6}$ again and again:

$$\frac{1}{6} \qquad \frac{2}{6} \qquad \frac{3}{6} \qquad \dots$$

```question
id: continue-the-pattern-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

After 3/6 comes {4/6|3/7|4/7}.

The pattern reaches one whole pizza at {6/6|6/1|1/6}.

After 6/6 comes {7/6|7/7|1/7}.
```

What could $\frac{7}{6}$ mean?

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Seven slices, each one sixth of a pizza. Six of them make one whole
pizza. The seventh slice is one sixth of a second pizza. So
$\frac{7}{6}$ is one whole pizza and one more sixth.

</details>

## 5. More than one pizza

What is $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{3}{4}$? How many whole pizzas is that, and
how much more?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. The slices are the same size: quarters. Add the tops.
2. One whole pizza is 4 quarters.
3. After one whole pizza, how many quarters are left?

**Think about:** what two quarters are the same amount as.

**Try this next:** what is 2/3 + 2/3?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

$\frac{3}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{4}$. Four of the quarters make
one whole pizza. Two quarters are left, which is half a pizza. So it is
one and a half pizzas. Python prints this amount as `3/2`: three
halves. That is the same amount, with a shorter name.

</details>

## 6. Into sixths

<img src="half-in-sixths.svg" alt="A fraction wall of three bars of the same length. The top bar is one whole. The next bar is cut into 2 halves, with 1 shaded. The bottom bar is cut into 6 sixths, with 3 shaded. The shaded parts of the two bars end at the same place.">

What is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. The slices are different sizes: a half and a sixth.
2. Use the wall. How many sixths is one half?
3. Now both are in sixths. Add the tops.

**Think about:** can your answer have a shorter name?

**Try this next:** what is 1/3 + 1/6?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

One half is $\frac{3}{6}$. So

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6}$$

$\frac{4}{6}$ has a shorter name: divide the top and the bottom by 2,
and get $\frac{2}{3}$.

</details>

## 7. Find a shared bottom number

What is $\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Thirds and quarters are different sizes. Find a slice size that
   works for both.
2. 12 slices can be put in 3 equal groups, and in 4 equal
   groups.
3. Name 2/3 in twelfths, and 1/4 in twelfths. Then add the tops.

**Think about:** why 7 does not work as the bottom number.

**Try this next:** what is 1/2 + 2/5?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

A common denominator is 12. Two thirds is $\frac{8}{12}$, and one
quarter is $\frac{3}{12}$. So

$$\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{8}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{11}{12}$$

That is one twelfth less than a whole pizza.

</details>

## 8. What went differently here?

Somebody wrote this:

$$\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{6}$$

$\frac{2}{6}$ has a shorter name. What is it? What does that tell you
about the sum? What went differently in their adding?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Divide the top and the bottom of 2/6 by 2.
2. Compare that with the 1/3 they started with.
3. Adding a third to a third should give more than a third.

**Think about:** what happened to the size of the slices when the
bottoms were added.

**Try this next:** what is 1/3 + 1/3, if the slices stay thirds?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one answer</summary>

$\frac{2}{6}$ is the same amount as $\frac{1}{3}$. So their sum says
that a third plus a third is a third. That cannot be, because we added
more pizza. They added the bottoms, 3 and 3. That made the slices
smaller, sixths. But the slices are still thirds. So
$\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$. Here is one answer. Yours may
be different and work too.

</details>

## 9. Two paths, one answer

Find $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4}$ in two different ways, and see if they
agree.

- **In quarters.** Name one half in quarters, then add.
- **In eighths.** Name both fractions in eighths, then add.

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. One half is 2 quarters. One half is also 4 eighths.
2. One quarter is 2 eighths.
3. Add the tops each time. Then compare the two answers.

**Think about:** do the two answers look the same? Are they the same
amount?

**Try this next:** find 1/2 + 1/4 in sixteenths.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

In quarters: $\frac{2}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$.

In eighths: $\frac{4}{8} + \frac{2}{8} = \frac{6}{8}$.

$\frac{6}{8}$ is the same amount as $\frac{3}{4}$: divide the top and
the bottom by 2. The two paths agree. The quarters path uses smaller
numbers.

</details>

## 11. A stretch: three slices that make a whole

What is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6}$? Find it by hand
first. Then check it with Python.

```python exec
id: three-slices-1
from fractions import Fraction

print(Fraction(1, 2) + Fraction(1, 3) + Fraction(1, 6))
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Sixths work for all three fractions.
2. One half is 3 sixths. One third is 2 sixths.
3. Add the three tops.

**Think about:** what 6/6 is.

**Try this next:** what is 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/4?

</details>

Here is the stretch. Can you find *four* different fractions, each with
1 on the top, that make exactly one whole pizza? Change the cell to
check your guesses.

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

In sixths, $\frac{3}{6} + \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{6}{6} = 1$.
Python prints `1`.

For four fractions, one answer is

$$\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{12} = 1$$

In twelfths, that is $6 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 12$ twelfths. There are other
answers too. More than 3000 years ago, people in Egypt wrote most
fractions as sums like these.

</details>

## 12. From earlier: which is bigger?

From *Comparing fractions: which is bigger?* Which is more pizza,
$\frac{3}{5}$ or $\frac{5}{8}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Both are more than one half, so the landmarks do not decide it.
2. Find a bottom number that works for fifths and for eighths. Try
   5 × 8.
3. Name both in fortieths, and compare the tops.

**Think about:** this is the same move as a common denominator for
adding.

**Try this next:** which is bigger, 2/3 or 5/7?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

$\frac{3}{5} = \frac{24}{40}$ and $\frac{5}{8} = \frac{25}{40}$. So
$\frac{5}{8}$ is bigger, by one fortieth.

</details>
