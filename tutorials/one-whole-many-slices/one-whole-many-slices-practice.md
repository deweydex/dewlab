# Fractions: one whole pizza, many slices — Practice

Small problems on one idea: all the slices of a pizza make the whole
pizza. Try each problem before you open anything under it. There is no
hurry, and no score. Some problems come with numbers, shapes or letters.
Choose the way you like in the box under the title.

## 1. Whole, or not?

```question
id: whole-or-not-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Which of these is exactly one whole pizza?

- 9/9
  - 9 slices out of the 9 the pizza was cut into: every slice.
- 9/1
  - The pizza was cut into 1 slice, and we have 9 of them. That is 9
    whole pizzas.
- 1/9
  - One thin slice, out of 9.
```

## 5. Nothing, a half, or a whole?

No counting needed here. Picture each pizza, and guess which of three
amounts it is closest to.

```question
id: nothing-half-or-whole-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

1/10 of a pizza is closest to {nothing|half a pizza|a whole pizza}.

9/10 of a pizza is closest to {a whole pizza|half a pizza|nothing}.

5/10 of a pizza is exactly {half a pizza|nothing|a whole pizza}.

99/100 of a pizza is closest to {a whole pizza|half a pizza|nothing}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Picture a pizza cut into 10 slices.
2. Shade 1 slice. Is most of the pizza shaded, or most of it empty?
3. Now shade 9. Which is most of it now?

**Think about:** how many slices are *missing* in each case.

**Try this next:** is 49/100 closer to nothing, a half or a whole?

</details>

## 6. A long sum

A pizza is cut into 7 slices, and we put all 7 back together:

$$\frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{7}$$

```question
id: a-long-sum-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

All seven slices make {1|7/49|7} whole pizza.
```

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Seven slices of one seventh are all the slices of one pizza. So the sum
is $\frac{7}{7} = 1$.

If you chose 7/49, you added the tops *and* the bottoms. Many people
guess this. But the slices are still sevenths, however many we have. So
the bottom number stays 7.

</details>

## 7. Very, very thin

A pizza is cut into a million equal slices. Each slice is
$\frac{1}{1000000}$ of the pizza. It is much too thin to see.

How many of those slices make the whole pizza? And if you had 999,999
of them, how much pizza would be missing?

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>answer</summary>

A million of them make the whole pizza. With 999,999, one slice is
missing: one millionth of a pizza, which nobody would notice.

</details>

## 8. The same, with more slices

Why is $\frac{12}{12}$ the same amount of pizza as $\frac{3}{3}$, when
12 is so much bigger than 3?

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one good answer</summary>

Both are every slice of one pizza. The pizza for 12/12 was cut into
more slices, so each slice is smaller. More slices, each smaller, make
the same whole. Your way of saying it may be clearer than this one.

</details>

## 9. What could 0/4 mean?

A pizza is cut into 4 slices, and you have none of them. How would you
write that as a fraction, and how much pizza is it?

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>answer</summary>

$\frac{0}{4}$: zero slices out of 4. It is no pizza at all, which is
the same as 0.

</details>

## 10. More than one pizza

This problem goes one step past the page. What could $\frac{5}{4}$
mean? Can you draw it?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. The bottom number, 4, says every pizza is cut into 4 slices.
2. The top number, 5, says we have 5 of those slices.
3. One pizza only has 4 slices. Where could the fifth come from?

**Think about:** what 4 of the 5 slices make on their own.

**Try this next:** what could $\frac{8}{4}$ mean?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Five quarter slices. Four of them make one whole pizza. The fifth slice
is one quarter of a second pizza. So $\frac{5}{4}$ is one whole pizza
and one more quarter. And $\frac{8}{4}$ is two whole pizzas.

</details>

## 11. From earlier: folding paper

From *Before we start*. A sheet of paper is folded in half 5 times. How
many layers thick is it now?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Before any folds, there is 1 layer.
2. Each fold doubles the layers.
3. Double 1, five times.

**Think about:** what one fold does to any number of layers.

**Try this next:** how many layers after 6 folds?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>answer</summary>

32 layers: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32.

</details>
