# Planning a site

What happens when we build five pages with no plan? They often disagree
with each other. One page has one heading style, and the next has a
different one. One page has no links to it at all. A plan written first
makes five pages feel like one site. On this page we:

- compare two plans for the same five pages
- meet three questions that do most of the planning work
- start the plan for your own site

## Two site maps

Here are two descriptions of the same five pages.

**The first one:**

> Page 1, page 2, page 3, page 4, page 5.

**The second one:**

| Page | What it is for | How a visitor reaches it |
|---|---|---|
| Home | Introduces the site | It links to the other four |
| About | The background a visitor needs before the rest makes sense | — |
| Gallery | Images | Linked from Home |
| Contact | A form | Linked from every page's navigation |
| Resources | Further links | Linked from About and Home |

Which one tells you more about the site?

The second one names what each page is for, and how a visitor reaches
it. That is a site map. A *site map* says what each page is for, and how
the pages connect. It is a list of jobs and connections.

## Three questions for a plan

A visitor never sees your plan. They see whether the site holds
together. Is the navigation the same on every page? Does a heading style
mean the same thing each time? Does the page they land on tell them
where to go next?

Planning is where we make those decisions, once. Without a plan, we
make them on every page, while we are also writing HTML.

Three questions do most of the work:

1. **Who is this for?** Picture a visitor who arrives with no context.
   In seconds, they decide whether to stay.
2. **What is each page for?** Think about its job, as well as its topic.
   What should a visitor do or learn there? Where should they go next?
3. **How do the pages connect?** Which pages link to which, and why? A
   page that nothing links to is a page that no visitor will find.

A fourth question is about layout: where does everything sit on the
page, roughly, before we decide exactly how it looks? A wireframe
answers it before any CSS exists. A *wireframe* is a rough sketch of a
page's layout.

## Plan your own site

Do these before you change any HTML file.

1. Open your copy of `project_wad`, and open `planning.md`.
2. Answer the questions about purpose and audience.
3. Fill in the site map table. What will each of your five pages be
   about?
4. Sketch a wireframe for at least one page, however rough. It does
   not have to look good. It only has to show where things go.

Can you say, for each of your pages, what it is for and which pages link
to it?

## What we have now

We now have a plan that a visitor never sees, and a site that holds
together because of it.

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *site map* | A description of what each page is for, and how the pages connect | "Contact: a form, linked from every page's navigation" |
| *wireframe* | A rough sketch of a page's layout, made before its CSS | boxes on paper for a header, a menu and content |
