# How this course is built

Dear reader,

Before you roll your eyes and get ready for another maths course that
feels like a long wait in a queue: this one is built differently, and I
would like to tell you how, and why. A course is full of choices. The
person learning almost never hears about them. If maths has gone badly
for you before, some of that came from choices nobody explained to you.
So here are mine, and you are allowed to judge them.

This page has no code and no questions. You can read it now, come back
later, or skip it. Nothing on a later page needs it.

## The usual way

You may know this shape already. Maths and programming are two
subjects, with different rooms and different books, and they rarely
talk to each other. Each topic gets a week: percentages, then
probability, then loops, and then the course moves on. The formula
comes first, and the reason comes later, if it comes at all. And a
wrong move gets a red cross, and then the next question.

For many people that works well enough. For someone who already
expects to fail, it does something quieter. A topic you meet once and
never need again tells you it was never for you. A formula with no
reason is one more thing to remember, and then one more thing to
forget. A red cross says "you are wrong", when it could have said
"here is what happened".

## What we do instead

**One subject, not two.** Maths and programming live on the same pages
here. A maths question needs a loop to add up a hundred numbers. A
program needs a logarithm to say why it is fast. When each subject
needs the other, neither one is a thing you learn and then leave behind.

**A question first.** Every page starts with something a person might
want to know: how long a signal takes to reach a satellite, or whether
a coin is fair. The maths and the code arrive because the question
needs them. We use ideas by feel first, and give them their proper
names afterwards.

**Four questions.** [Four questions for any puzzle](tutorial:four-questions)
gave you four questions: What is named here? What is promised? What
happens when? What does this space let us do? They come back on every
page, so you never start from nothing.

**"What space are we in?" instead of "that's wrong".** On a clock,
10 + 4 is 2. In the counting numbers, 3 − 5 has no answer. When a move
does not work, we ask where it would work. A lot of "wrong" moves are
right somewhere, and finding out where is some of the best maths there
is. (Many of us were told at school that 4 − 5 was just "not allowed". It
was allowed all along. Nobody had shown us the space where it worked.)

**Guess before you run.** Before a cell shows you something new, the
page asks you to guess what will happen. You will often be wrong. So am
I. A wrong guess is the most useful kind: it points at the exact idea to
look at again, and nobody marks it.

**Error messages on day one.** Red text will appear on your screen in
the first hour anyway, so [When Python says no](tutorial:when-python-says-no)
reads it with you early. An error message is Python telling you which
move it could not make, and where. It is information about one line of
code. It says nothing about you.

**Everything comes back.** Every page starts with two short questions
from earlier pages, and every unit ends with a page of mixed problems.
Powers come back when we count bits. Truth tables come back when we
count outfits.

**A toolkit of your own.** You write small functions, like `split_bill`
and `factorial`, and later pages load them for you. Last week's work is
what this week's page stands on. If one of yours is missing, or has a
mistake in it, the page quietly uses a reference version, so nothing
stops working.

**Your way of writing it down counts.** If you invent your own names or
your own notation, that is fine, as long as someone else can follow it.
Many problems have more than one good answer, and the answer in the
fold is one of them, not the only one. Yours may well be more
interesting.

## What it costs

These choices have costs, and it is only fair to name them.

**It is slower in places.** Counting every outfit with a loop, and then
checking the formula against the list, takes longer than being handed
$m \times n$. Writing `total` yourself takes longer than using Python's
own `sum()`.

**Some things arrive later than a textbook would put them,** and some
arrive earlier. Lists get their own page in Unit 5, but pages use them
before that. Logarithms show up in the first unit. Meeting an idea
before its own page can feel strange.

**Some people prefer the formula first.** That is a reasonable way to
learn. If it is yours, the formula is always on the page, on a line of
its own, and you can start from there.

**It will hurt your brain sometimes.** Some questions ask you to explain
why something is true, not only to get the number. That is harder, and
it is meant to be. The struggle is real, and it is also where the
learning happens. If it feels like hard work, that is the right
feeling.

## Tell me when it is not working

Every tutorial has a closed box near the end, "Why this way?". It names
one choice that page made and what it could have done instead. Open it
when you are curious. Some practice pages also ask how *you* would have
taught something. Your own experience of learning counts as evidence
there.

If the way this course teaches is not working for you, please say so.
Tell your teacher, or use the link at the bottom of any page: open
"Something wrong on this page? Tell us." and choose "The page is wrong,
or I could not follow it". The same choices are behind the round button
with a flag on it, in the corner of the screen. They go to GitHub, which
asks you to sign in with a free account first.

"I could not follow it" is a real report, and I will treat it as one.
When a page loses a reader, it is the page that needs fixing.

The course is called the Dewey Track, after John Dewey, who argued that
people learn by doing. Here, you learn by dewing. The next page,
[Recipes are algorithms](tutorial:recipes-are-algorithms), is where the
dewing starts.
