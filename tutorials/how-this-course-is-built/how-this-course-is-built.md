# How this course is built

This page is a letter from the people who made this course. It has no
code and no questions. It says how the course is built: what we chose,
what we turned down, and what our choices cost. You can read it now,
come back to it later, or skip it. Nothing on a later page depends on it.

We are writing it because a course is full of choices, and the person
learning usually never hears about them. If maths has gone badly for you
before, some of that came from choices that nobody explained to you. So
here are ours, and you can judge them.

## The usual way

A course like this one usually has a shape. You may know it already.

Maths and programming are two subjects. They have different teachers,
different rooms and different books, and they seldom mention each other.

Each topic is taught once. There is a week on percentages, a week on
probability, a week on loops, and then the course moves on.

The formula comes first. You are given $A = \pi r^2$, and some examples
that use it. The reason for the formula comes later, if it comes at all.

A wrong move is marked wrong. There is a red cross, and then the next
question.

For many people this shape works well enough. For someone who already
expects to fail, it does something that is harder to see. A topic met
once and never needed again tells them it was never for them. A formula
without a reason is one more thing to remember, and one more thing to
forget. And a red cross, with nothing after it, says "you are wrong",
when it could have said "here is what happened".

## What this course does instead

Each of these is a choice, and each one has a reason.

**One subject, not two.** Maths and programming are taught together, on
the same pages. A maths question needs a loop to add up a hundred
numbers. A program needs a logarithm to say why it is fast. When each
subject needs the other, neither is a topic you learn and then leave
behind.

**A question from the world first.** Every page starts with something a
person might want to know: what a year of coffee costs, or whether a
coin is fair. The maths and the code arrive because the question needs
them.

**Four questions.** [Four questions for any puzzle](tutorial:four-questions)
gave you four questions. What is named here? What is promised? What
happens when? What does this space let us do? They come back on every
page. They work on a board game, a recipe, a line of Python and a
formula, so you never start from nothing.

**"What space are we in?", in place of "that's wrong".** On a clock,
10 + 4 is 2. In the counting numbers, 3 − 5 has no answer. When a move
does not work, we ask which space it would work in. Many "wrong" moves
are right somewhere, and finding out where is part of the maths.

**Predict before you run.** Before a cell shows something new, the page
asks you to guess. Many guesses will be wrong, and that is expected. A
wrong guess is where the learning is: it shows you which idea to look at
again. Nobody marks your guess.

**Error messages early.** [When Python says no](tutorial:when-python-says-no)
comes in the first unit. Red text will appear on your first day anyway,
so we read it together, early. An error message is the machine telling
you which move it could not make, and where. It is information about one
line, and it is not a mark against you.

**Every topic comes back.** Every page starts with two warm-up questions
from earlier pages, and every unit ends with a page of mixed problems.
Powers come back when we count bits. Truth tables come back when we
count outfits. Nothing is taught once and left.

**A toolkit of your own.** From
[Numbers a computer can hold](tutorial:numbers-a-computer-can-hold)
onwards, you write small functions, like `split_bill` and `factorial`,
and later pages load them for you. Last week's work is not thrown away.
It is what this week's page is built on. If one of your functions is
missing, or has a mistake in it, the page uses a reference version in
its place, so no page stops working.

## What it costs

These choices have costs, and it is fair to say what they are.

**It is slower in places.** Counting every outfit with a loop, and then
checking the formula against the list, takes longer than being given
$m \times n$ and trusting it. Writing `total` yourself takes longer than
using Python's own `sum()`.

**Some things arrive later than a textbook would put them.** Lists, for
example, wait for Unit 5. Until then, a page uses a list here and there,
and says that the full story comes later. Many textbooks teach lists in
the first few weeks.

**Some things arrive earlier than you might expect.** Loops appear in
Unit 2, a unit before they are taught. Logarithms appear in the first
unit. Meeting an idea before its own page can feel strange, and some
readers would rather meet each idea once, in full.

**Some readers would prefer the formula first.** That is a reasonable
preference. If you learn best from a rule and then examples, the lists
and the loops can feel like extra steps. The formula is always on the
page, usually on a line of its own, and you can start from there.

**The four questions take room.** Every page ends by asking them again.
For some readers that helps to tie the page together. For others it is
repetition.

We made these choices for readers who have had a hard time with maths
or computers before. They will not suit everybody, and they are not the
only good way to teach.

## Tell us when it is not working

Each tutorial has a closed box near its end, called "Why this way?". It
names one choice that page made, and what else it could have done. Open
it when you are curious, and skip it when you are not. Some practice
pages also ask which way you would have taught something. Those
questions have no single right answer, and your own experience of
learning counts as evidence.

If the way this course teaches is not working for you, please say so.
You can tell your teacher. Or you can use the link at the bottom of any
page: open "Something wrong on this page? Tell us." and choose "The page
is wrong, or I could not follow it". The same three choices are behind
the round button with a flag on it, in the corner of the screen. They go to
GitHub, which asks you to sign in with a free account first.

"I could not follow it" is a real report, and we treat it as one. When a
page loses a reader, it is the page that needs fixing.

starts the work.
