# Scientific notation: powers of ten, from a virus to Voyager

This is the last page of the part of the module on powers. It uses
powers of ten to measure very small things and very big things.

Here are seven boxes, from $10^{-3}$ to $10^3$. Each box shows its
power of ten, and the same number written out.

<img src="ten-ladder.svg" alt="Seven boxes in a row. From left to right they show 10 to the power −3, which is 0.001; 10 to the power −2, which is 0.01; 10 to the power −1, which is 0.1; 10 to the power 0, which is 1, in a coloured box; 10 to the power 1, which is 10; 10 to the power 2, which is 100; and 10 to the power 3, which is 1,000. Under the boxes: each box is ×10 the one on its left.">

```question
id: seven-boxes-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 2

How many steps of ×10 go from the first box, 0.001, to the last box,
1,000?

- 7
  - There are seven boxes. The steps are between the boxes.
- 6
  - From −3 to 3 is six steps: −3 to −2, −2 to −1, and so on, up to 2
    to 3.
- 3
  - Three steps go from 1 to 1,000. The row also goes three steps
    below 1.
```

## Big numbers, short

3000 is 3 × 1000. And 1000 is $10^3$. So

$$3000 = 3 \times 10^3$$

```question

{10⁶|10⁵|10⁷}.
```

3. How many steps of ×10 go from 1 to 1,000,000?

**Think about:** the power of ten counts the zeros.

## Numbers that are not round

7300 is 7.3 × 1000. So $7300 = 7.3 \times 10^3$. The point moves
three places when we multiply by $10^3$.

```question

2,300,000 = 2.3 ×
{10⁶|10⁵|10⁷}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

2. Multiply by 10 again and again: 23, 230, 2300, …
3. Count the steps until you land on 2,300,000.

**Think about:** each step of ×10 moves the point one place to the
right.

**Try this next:** 4,560 = 4.56 × 10 to what power?

</details>

## A name for this way of writing

Here are three ways to write 1234.

$$1.234 \times 10^3 \qquad 12.34 \times 10^2 \qquad 0.1234 \times 10^4$$

All three are the same number. People usually choose the first one.
It has exactly one digit, not 0, before the point. This way of writing a
number is called *scientific notation*.

```question
id: a-name-for-this-way-of-writing-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 3

Which of these is 2,300,000 in scientific notation?

- 23 × 10⁵
  - This is 2,300,000 too. But it has two digits before the point.
- 0.23 × 10⁷
  - This is 2,300,000 too. But the digit before the point is 0.
- 2.3 × 10⁶
  - One digit, 2, before the point, times a power of ten.
```

## The mass of the Earth

*Mass* is how much stuff is in a thing. We measure it in kilograms
(kg). NASA gives the mass of the Earth as

$$5.9722 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$$

The long way, it is 5,972,200,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg.

```question
id: the-mass-of-the-earth-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

The long way has 25 digits: a 5, then 24 more. So the power, 24,
counts
{the digits after the first one|all the digits|the zeros only}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Look at 7300 = 7.3 × 10³. How many digits come after the 7 in 7300?
2. Now look at 5,000,000 = 5 × 10⁶. How many digits come after the 5?
3. Is the count the same as the power each time?

**Think about:** each step of ×10 moves the point one place. So each
step puts one more digit after the first one.

**Try this next:** how many zeros are in the long way of the Earth's
mass?

</details>

Nobody wants to count 24 digits. Scientific notation counts them for
us.

## Small numbers, short

Now the left side of the row of boxes. From *Negative powers: more on
the bottom*, $10^{-3}$ is 1/1000, or 0.001. A millimetre is
$10^{-3}$ metres.

A *nanometre* (nm) is much smaller. It is $10^{-9}$ metres, a
billionth of a metre. Many viruses are between 20 and 300 nm across.
A small virus is about 20 nm across:

$$20 \text{ nm} = 20 \times 10^{-9} \text{ m}$$

```question
id: small-numbers-short-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

20 is 2 × 10. So 20 × 10⁻⁹ is 2 × 10 × 10⁻⁹, which is 2 ×
{10⁻⁸|10⁻⁹|10⁻¹⁰}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Look at the row of boxes. Multiplying by 10 moves one box to the
   right.
2. Which box is one to the right of $10^{-3}$? It is $10^{-2}$.
3. In the same way, which power is one to the right of $10^{-9}$?

**Think about:** joining two stacks adds the powers: 1 + (−9).

**Try this next:** 300 nm is 3 × 10 to what power, in metres?

</details>

So a small virus is $2 \times 10^{-8}$ metres across. For a number less
than 1, the power is negative.

## Viruses in a row

A millimetre is $10^{-3}$ m, and a nanometre is $10^{-9}$ m. On the
row of boxes, six steps of ×10 go from $10^{-9}$ to $10^{-3}$. So one
millimetre is $10^6$ nanometres: 1,000,000 nm.

```question
id: viruses-in-a-row-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

A small virus is 20 nm across. So in a row across one millimetre, there
are 1,000,000 ÷ 20 =
{50,000|5,000|500,000}
viruses.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. 1,000,000 ÷ 10 is 100,000.
2. 20 is 2 × 10. So divide 100,000 by 2 as well.
3. What is half of 100,000?

**Think about:** dividing by 20 is dividing by 10, then by 2.

**Try this next:** how many 300 nm viruses fit across one millimetre?

</details>

A full stop at the end of a sentence is smaller than a millimetre. Say
it is 0.1 mm across. (That is a guess. Hold a ruler next to one.) Then
about 5,000 small viruses fit across it, in a row.

## Multiplying

In *Multiplying powers: joining two stacks*, multiplying two powers of
ten added the powers: $10^3 \times 10^2 = 10^5$. Scientific notation
uses this. Multiply the front numbers, and add the powers:

$$(2 \times 10^3) \times (3 \times 10^2) = (2 \times 3) \times (10^3 \times 10^2) = 6 \times 10^5$$

## When the front gets too big

$(8 \times 10^8) \times (7 \times 10^7)$ is $56 \times 10^{15}$. But
56 has two digits before the point. So it is not in scientific
notation. 56 is 5.6 × 10, and that extra 10 joins the other tens.

```question
id: when-the-front-gets-too-big-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

56 × 10¹⁵ = 5.6 × 10 × 10¹⁵ = 5.6 ×
{10¹⁶|10¹⁵|10¹⁴}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. 56 is 5.6 × 10.
2. So there is one more 10 to join with $10^{15}$.
3. How many tens are there now?

**Think about:** the point moved one place left, so the power goes up
by one.

**Try this next:** 12 × 10³ in scientific notation.

</details>

## Python writes it too

In Python, `2.59e10` means $2.59 \times 10^{10}$. The e stands for
"times ten to the power". Python writes very big numbers this way
itself.

```python exec
id: python-writes-it-too-1
print(2.59e10)
print(5.9722e24)
```

The first line prints 25900000000.0, the long way. The second prints
5.9722e+24. The number is too long, so Python keeps the short way.

Now we check NASA's mass of the Earth. NASA also gives the Earth's
*volume*, the space it takes up. In cubic metres, it is
$1.08321 \times 10^{21}$. On average, each cubic metre of the Earth has
5513 kg of stuff in it. This is called the Earth's *density*. So the
mass is the volume times 5513.

In the cell, `f"{mass:.2e}"` writes the mass in scientific notation,
with 2 digits after the point.

```python exec
id: python-writes-it-too-2
volume = 1.08321e21    # cubic metres
density = 5.513e3      # kilograms in each cubic metre
mass = density * volume
print(f"{mass:.2e}")
```

```predict
type: choice

Before you run it: which power of ten will the mass have?

- 10²⁴
- 10⁶³
  - 3 × 21 is 63. Multiplying the powers can feel natural here.
- 10²¹
  - The volume has 10²¹. But the density has a power of ten too.
```

It prints 5.97e+24. That is $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg, the same as
NASA's mass. The powers added: 3 + 21 = 24. And 5.513 × 1.08321 is
about 5.97.

## Light

Nothing travels faster than light. It travels 299,792,458 metres in
one second. In kilometres (km), that is 299,792.458 km in a second. A
kilometre is 1000 metres.

```question
id: light-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

299,792.458 is about 3.00 ×
{10⁵|10⁶|10³}.

So light travels about 3.00 × 10⁵ km in one second.
```

In one minute, light travels 60 times as far:

$$3.00 \times 10^5 \times 60 = 180 \times 10^5 = 1.8 \times 10^7 \text{ km}$$

This distance is called a *light-minute*. It is a distance, even
though its name has a time in it.

## The Sun

The Earth is about 149.6 million km from the Sun, on average. That is
$1.496 \times 10^8$ km. This distance is called an *astronomical unit*,
or AU.

```question
id: the-sun-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Light travels 1.8 × 10⁷ km in a minute. About how long does light from
the Sun take to reach us?

- About 8 minutes
  - 1.496 × 10⁸ is about 150 million, and 1.8 × 10⁷ is 18 million.
    150 ÷ 18 is about 8.
- About 8 seconds
  - One light-minute is 18 million km. The Sun is much further than
    that.
- About 8 hours
  - 8 light-hours would be 60 times as far as 8 light-minutes.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

2. How many 18 millions fit in about 150 millions?
3. Each one is a minute of travel for light.

**Think about:** this is the question "how many fit?" from *Dividing
fractions: how many fit?*

**Try this next:** Mars is about 228.0 million km from the Sun. About

</details>

When you see the Sun, you see light that left it about 8 minutes ago.

## Voyager 1

Voyager 1 is a spacecraft. It left Earth in 1977, and it is still
travelling away from us. How far does light travel in one day? That
distance is a *light-day*.

A day has 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 seconds.

```python exec
id: voyager-1-1
speed = 299792.458          # km in each second
seconds_in_a_day = 24 * 60 * 60
light_day = speed * seconds_in_a_day
print(f"{light_day:.4e}")
```

```predict
type: choice

Before you run it: about how many km is one light-day?

- About 2.6 × 10¹⁰
- About 2.6 × 10⁸
  - One light-minute is already 1.8 × 10⁷ km. A day has 1440 minutes.
- About 2.6 × 10²⁰
  - 10⁵ × 10⁴ adds the powers. It does not multiply them.
```

It prints 2.5902e+10. That is $2.5902 \times 10^{10}$ km, about 25.9
billion km. By hand, with the rounded speed:

$$3.00 \times 10^5 \times 8.64 \times 10^4 = 25.92 \times 10^9 = 2.592 \times 10^{10}$$

NASA says that on 18 November 2026, Voyager 1 will be 25.902 billion km
from Earth. That is one light-day. Nothing made by people has been so
far away before. It is about 173 AU: 173 times as far as the Sun.

## Your rule, in your words

Before you read our version, say in your own words how scientific
notation writes a number, and how to multiply two numbers written that
way. Write it in the Notes panel or on paper, or say it aloud.

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way to say it</summary>

Scientific notation writes a number as one digit, then a point and more
digits, times a power of ten. The power counts how many places the
point moves. It is positive for big numbers and negative for numbers
less than 1. To multiply two of them, multiply the front numbers and
add the powers. If the front gets to 10 or more, move the point one
place and add 1 to the power. Your way of saying it may be clearer than
ours.

</details>

## Looking back

A virus is about $2 \times 10^{-8}$ m across. Voyager 1 will be about
$2.6 \times 10^{13}$ m away. Why is scientific notation useful for both
numbers?

A challenge: the program below finds how far light travels in one
year, a *light-year*. The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima
Centauri, is about 4.25 light-years away. How far is that in km? How
many light-days is it? Voyager 1 took about 49 years to go one
light-day. At that speed, how long would it take to reach Proxima
Centauri?

```python challenge
# How far light travels in one year, in km.
speed = 299792.458          # km in each second
seconds_in_a_year = 365.25 * 24 * 60 * 60
light_year = speed * seconds_in_a_year
print(f"{light_year:.2e}")
```
