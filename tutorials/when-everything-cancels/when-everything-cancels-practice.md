# The zero power: when everything cancels — Practice

Small problems on one idea. When the top and the bottom have the same
tiles, every tile cancels. The answer is 1, so a zero power is 1.
Try each problem before you open anything under it. Choose numbers,
shapes or letters in the box under the title.

<div class="dl-world" data-world="numbers">

## 5. Put the tops together

This one has two fractions, multiplied. Put all the tops together, and
all the bottoms together.

<div class="dl-world" data-world="numbers">

```question
id: put-the-tops-together-1--numbers
type: fill-in-the-blank

In (2²/2) × (2³/2⁴), the tops are 2² × 2³, which is
{2⁵|2⁶|4⁵}.

The bottoms are 2 × 2⁴, which is
{2⁵|2⁴|4⁵}.

So the whole thing is
{1|0|2}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. When two fractions are multiplied, the tops multiply together, and
   the bottoms multiply together.
2. A power on its own, like ♡, is ♡¹: one tile.
3. Join the tops into one row, and the bottoms into one row. Then
   compare.

**Think about:** the second fraction has more on the bottom. If we join
everything first, we never divide with more on the bottom.

## 8. What went differently here?

Somebody wrote 7⁰ = 0. They said: "The exponent is 0, so there are no
sevens. So it is nothing." What would you show them?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Where does 7⁰ come from? Think of a division that leaves 7⁰.
2. Try 7²/7². Write it the long way, and cross out the pairs.
3. What is each pair worth?

**Think about:** a crossed-out pair is worth 1, not 0.

**Try this next:** somebody else wrote 7⁰ = 7. What would you show
them?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Show them 7²/7². The rule gives 7²⁻² = 7⁰. Crossing out gives two
pairs, each worth 1. And 7²/7² is 49/49, which is 1. So 7⁰ = 1. You
could also show them the pattern. 7² = 49 and 7¹ = 7. One more step
down divides by 7 again, which gives 1. "No sevens, so nothing" feels
natural. The pictures and the pattern both say 1.

</details>

## 10. A negative number or a fraction

This one goes a step past the page. Does everything still cancel when
the base is −2? Or when the base is ⅓? The cell checks
(−2)⁵/(−2)⁵ and (⅓)⁷/(⅓)⁷. Its first line gets Python's fraction tool
ready.

```python exec
id: a-negative-number-or-a-fraction-1
from fractions import Fraction

third = Fraction(1, 3)
print((-2) ** 5 / (-2) ** 5)
print(third ** 7 / third ** 7)
print((-2) ** 0, third ** 0)
```

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Both divisions are 1. The first prints 1.0, because Python writes the
answer to a `/` division with a decimal point. (−2)⁵ is −32, and −32
divided by −32 is 1. The last line prints 1 twice: the zero powers of
−2 and ⅓ are both 1. Every tile still has a partner, whatever number is
on it.

</details>

## 11. Make it messy

Can you make a very messy division that is exactly 1? Use many stacks,
big exponents, or two kinds of tile. Then give it to somebody, and ask
them to find it.

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Choose some stacks for the top. Add their exponents.
2. Choose different stacks for the bottom, with the same total.
3. Make it look scary. Mix the order, or use big numbers.

**Think about:** only the totals on the top and bottom need to match.

**Try this next:** make one with three fractions multiplied together.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

(♡¹⁰ × ♡²⁵ × ♡¹⁵)/(♡²⁰ × ♡³⁰). The top joins into ♡⁵⁰, and the bottom
joins into ♡⁵⁰. Every tile cancels, so it is 1. Here is one answer.
Yours may be different and work too.

</details>
