## 3. Closer to 0, a half or 1?

No counting needed here. Picture each pizza, and find the landmark it
is closest to.

```question

```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

**Think about:** a fraction is exactly one half when its top is half of
its bottom.

## 4. Which is closer to one half?

```question
id: closer-to-one-half-1
type: multiple-choice
answer: 1

Which of these is closest to one half: 2/5, 3/4 or 1/10?

- 2/5
  - Half of 5 is two and a half, and the top is 2. That is close.
- 3/4
  - Half of 4 is 2, and the top is 3. That is a whole slice away, and
    a quarter is a big slice.
- 1/10
  - 1/10 is a small amount. It is much closer to 0 than to one half.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Put the three fractions on the empty line from the tutorial, in
   your head or on paper.
2. For each one, how far is it from the tick for one half?
3. To measure the gaps, name everything in twentieths. One half is
   10/20.

**Think about:** how many twentieths each fraction is from 10/20.

**Try this next:** which is closer to one half, 5/8 or 1/3?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

In twentieths, one half is 10/20.

- 2/5 is 8/20. That is 2 twentieths from one half.
- 3/4 is 15/20. That is 5 twentieths from one half.
- 1/10 is 2/20. That is 8 twentieths from one half.

So 2/5 is the closest.

</details>

## 7. Put them in order

Here are four amounts of pizza:

$$\frac{3}{4} \qquad \frac{1}{2} \qquad \frac{2}{3} \qquad \frac{5}{12}$$

Can you put them in order, smallest first?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Look at the bottom numbers: 4, 2, 3 and 12.
2. A pizza cut into 12 slices can be put in 4 equal groups, or 2,
   or 3. So twelfths work for all four fractions.
3. Name each fraction in twelfths. Then count.

**Think about:** which fraction is less than one half, and which are
more.

**Try this next:** where would 7/12 go in your order?

</details>

When you have an order, you can check it with Python. `sorted` puts the
fractions in order, smallest first. The last two lines print them one
at a time.

```python exec
id: put-them-in-order-1
from fractions import Fraction

amounts = [Fraction(3, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(2, 3), Fraction(5, 12)]
for amount in sorted(amounts):
    print(amount)
```

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

In twelfths, the four fractions are 9/12, 6/12, 8/12 and 5/12. So the
order, smallest first, is

$$\frac{5}{12} \qquad \frac{1}{2} \qquad \frac{2}{3} \qquad \frac{3}{4}$$

</details>

## 8. What went differently here?

Somebody compared $\frac{5}{9}$ and $\frac{2}{3}$. They said: "5 is
bigger than 2, and 9 is bigger than 3. So 5/9 is bigger." Which one is
more pizza? Why might they have thought it was 5/9?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. The slices are ninths and thirds. They are different sizes.
2. Cut every third into 3 smaller slices. Now it is counted in ninths.
3. How many ninths is 2/3?

**Think about:** what a bigger bottom number does to each slice.

**Try this next:** which is bigger, 7/10 or 4/5?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one answer</summary>

2/3 is the same amount as 6/9. Six ninths is more than five ninths. So
2/3 is more pizza, by one ninth. Bigger numbers can feel like more. But
the 9 on the bottom makes each slice smaller, and that matters too.
Here is one answer. Yours may be different and work too.

</details>

## 9. Two paths, one answer

Which is bigger, $\frac{3}{8}$ or $\frac{4}{7}$? Try two different
ways, and see if they agree.

- **With the landmarks.** Is each fraction more than one half, or less?
- **With the same bottom number.** Give both fractions a name with 56 on
  the bottom. (56 is 8 × 7.)

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Half of 8 is 4. Is 3 more than 4, or less?
2. Half of 7 is three and a half. Is 4 more than that, or less?
3. For 56ths, multiply the top and the bottom of 3/8 by 7. Multiply the
   top and the bottom of 4/7 by 8.

**Think about:** which path was quicker here. Would it be quicker for
every pair?

**Try this next:** which is bigger, 5/8 or 4/7? Do the landmarks help
this time?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

With the landmarks: 3/8 is less than one half, because 3 is less than
4. And 4/7 is more than one half, because 4 is more than three and a
half. So 4/7 is bigger.

With 56ths: 3/8 is 21/56, and 4/7 is 32/56. 32 is more than 21. So
4/7 is bigger. The two paths agree.

</details>

## 11. A stretch: continue the pattern

$$\frac{1}{2} \qquad \frac{2}{3} \qquad \frac{3}{4} \qquad \frac{4}{5} \qquad \dots$$

```question
id: continue-the-pattern-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

After 4/5 comes {5/6|5/5|4/6}.
```

Each fraction in this row is one slice less than a whole pizza. Does
each one get bigger, or smaller? Then use that to answer this: which is
bigger, $\frac{99}{100}$ or $\frac{100}{101}$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. 1/2 has one half missing. 2/3 has one third missing. 3/4 has one
   quarter missing.
2. Which missing slice is the biggest? Which is the smallest?
3. A smaller missing slice means the fraction is closer to 1.

**Think about:** how big the missing slice is for 99/100, and for
100/101.

**Try this next:** will a fraction in this row ever be exactly 1?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

Each fraction is bigger than the one before. The missing slice gets
smaller each time: one half, one third, one quarter, one fifth. So the
fractions get closer to 1.

99/100 has 1/100 missing. 100/101 has 1/101 missing, which is a thinner
slice. So 100/101 is bigger. It is closer to 1.

</details>

## 13. From earlier: halves of halves

From *Powers: the long way and the short way*. Which is bigger,
$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2$ or $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3$?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. Picture the folded paper. After 2 folds, how many equal pieces are
   there?
2. After 3 folds, how many?
3. With more pieces in the same sheet, is each piece bigger or
   smaller?

**Think about:** what one more fold does to the size of each piece.

**Try this next:** which is bigger, $\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^2$ or
$\frac{1}{3}$?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>answer</summary>

$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$ and
$\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8}$. A quarter is bigger than an
eighth. So $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2$ is bigger.

</details>
