# Mixed problems: the whole of the Zen of Slashes and Surds

These problems use the whole module: fractions, powers, square roots
and logarithms, pictures and graphs, and the balance. They are mixed on
purpose. Each problem needs ideas from two parts of the module at once.
A problem does not say which parts. Choosing is part of the problem.

Try each problem before you open anything under it. There is no hurry,
and no score. Under each problem, "stuck? here are some steps" says
which pages the ideas come from. When you are not sure, draw it: a
pizza, a row of hearts, a line of hops, a grid or a balance. Some
problems come with numbers, shapes or letters. Choose the way you like
in the box under the title.

After 5 folds, each piece is $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^5 =
\frac{1}{32}$ of the sheet. That is $2^{-5}$.

## 2. Partner or opposite?

Two words in this module sound alike, and mean different things.

- A fraction's *partner* is the fraction turned over. 5/7 and 7/5 are
  partners, because 5/7 × 7/5 = 1.
- A number's *opposite* is the same distance from 0, on the other side.
  3 and −3 are opposites.

<div class="dl-world" data-world="numbers">

```question
id: partner-or-opposite-1--numbers
type: fill-in-the-blank

The partner of 2/3 is
{3/2|−2/3|1/3}.

The opposite of 2/3 is
{−2/3|3/2|1/3}.

2/3 × 3/2 is
{1|0|4/9}.

Start at 2/3 on the number line, and move 2/3 of a step to the left.
You land on
{0|1|−2/3}.

On the number line, the partner, 3/2, sits between
{1 and 2|0 and 1|−1 and 0}.

The opposite, −2/3, sits between
{−1 and 0|0 and 1|−2 and −1}.
```

</div>

<div class="dl-world" data-world="squiggles">

```question

{♡/△|−△/♡|1/♡}.

The opposite of △/♡ is
{−△/♡|♡/△|1/△}.

△/♡ × ♡/△ is
{1|0|△/♡}.

You land on
{0|1|−△/♡}.

{left|right}
of 0.
```

</div>

```question
{b/a|−a/b|1/b}.

{−a/b|b/a|1/a}.

{1|0|a/b}.

Start at a/b on the number line, and move a/b of a step to the left.

{0|1|−a/b}.

{left|right}
of 0.
```

</div>

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *Dividing fractions: how many fit?* and
   *The number line: whole numbers, fractions and negatives*.
2. For the partner, turn the fraction over. The top goes to the
   bottom, and the bottom goes to the top.
3. For the opposite, keep the fraction, and put a minus sign in front.
   It sits as far from 0, on the left.

**Think about:** which word is about multiplying to make 1? Which word
is about the number line?

**Try this next:** what are the partner and the opposite of 4?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

The partner of $\frac{2}{3}$ is $\frac{3}{2}$, and
$\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{6}{6} = 1$. The opposite of
$\frac{2}{3}$ is $-\frac{2}{3}$. A move of $\frac{2}{3}$ to the left
from $\frac{2}{3}$ lands on 0. $\frac{3}{2}$ is one and a half, between
1 and 2. $-\frac{2}{3}$ is between −1 and 0.

Partners multiply to make 1. Opposites are the same distance from 0.
The partner of 4 is $\frac{1}{4}$, and its opposite is −4.

</details>

## 3. A box with a power in it

On the balance pages, a box held a number we could not see. Here the
box sits in a power.

```question
id: a-box-with-a-power-in-it-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

In □² = 49, the box holds
{7|24.5|98}.

In □³ = 64, the box holds
{4|8|21 and a third}.

In $10^\square = 10000$, the box holds
{4|1000|5}.

In $2^\square = 32$, the box holds
{5|16|6}.

In $2^\square = 1/8$, the box holds
{−3|3|1/3}.
```

A stretch: in □² = 49, one more number fits, if the box may hold a
number below 0. Which number is it?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *Unknowns: a box with something in it*,
   *Undoing a square: square roots, the side of a square*, *Counting
   hops: logarithms, how many times did we multiply?* and *Negative
   powers: more on the bottom*.
2. When the box is under the power, it asks which number was
   multiplied. That is side or edge.
3. When the box is the exponent, it asks how many times. That is hops
   or folds. A guess and a check works for all of them.

**Think about:** 1/8 is 1 over 2³.

**Try this next:** $10^\square = 1/100$.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

- In □² = 49, the box holds side(49), which is 7.
- In □³ = 64, the box holds edge(64), which is 4.
- In $10^\square = 10000$, the box holds hops(10 → 10,000), which is 4.
- In $2^\square = 32$, the box holds folds(32), which is 5.
- $\frac{1}{8}$ is $2^{-3}$, so in $2^\square = 1/8$, the box holds −3.

For the stretch: −7 fits too, because −7 × −7 is 49. 7 and −7 are
opposites. A box of beads holds 7, because nobody can have −7 beads.

</details>

## 5. A chain in powers of ten

In 2016, the scientists Adam Frank and Woody Sullivan counted the stars
in the whole *observable Universe*: all the space we can see with
telescopes. They used about $2 \times 10^{22}$ stars. For every five
stars, they counted about one planet in a *habitable zone*, where water
can be a liquid.

Suppose one of those planets in 60 billion ever builds machines like
ours. 60 billion is $6 \times 10^{10}$. How many worlds in the whole
observable Universe would that be? Here is the chain:

$$2 \times 10^{22} \times \frac{1}{5} \times \frac{1}{6 \times 10^{10}}$$

```question
id: a-chain-in-powers-of-ten-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

2 × 10²² × 1/5 is 2/5 × 10²². 2/5 is 0.4, so this is
{4 × 10²¹|4 × 10²³|0.4 × 10²¹}.

To divide 4 × 10²¹ by 6 × 10¹⁰, we divide 4 by 6, and 10²¹ by 10¹⁰.
10²¹ ÷ 10¹⁰ is
{10¹¹|10³¹|10²¹⁰}.

4 ÷ 6 is 2/3, which is about
{0.67|1.5|−2}.

So the end of the chain is about 0.67 × 10¹¹, which is
{6.7 × 10¹⁰|6.7 × 10¹¹|6.7 × 10¹²}.
```

The cell checks the chain. The first line gets Python's fraction tool,
`Fraction`, ready. `float` writes a fraction as a decimal.

```python exec
id: a-chain-in-powers-of-ten-2
from fractions import Fraction

planets = 2 * 10 ** 22 * Fraction(1, 5)
worlds = planets * Fraction(1, 6 * 10 ** 10)
print(planets)
print(float(worlds))
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *The Drake equation: narrowing it down, one
   fraction at a time*, *Scientific notation: powers of ten, from a
   virus to Voyager* and *Dividing powers: crossing out pairs*.
2. 0.4 × 10²² has a front number less than 1. Move the point one place
   to the right, and take 1 from the power.
3. To divide two powers of 10, take the bottom exponent away from the
   top one: 21 − 10.

**Think about:** each fraction in a chain narrows the count. Is the end
still a big number?

**Try this next:** suppose the chance were one in $4 \times 10^{21}$.
How many worlds would that be?

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

$2 \times 10^{22} \times \frac{1}{5} = 0.4 \times 10^{22} = 4 \times
10^{21}$ planets. Then $\frac{4}{6} \times 10^{11}$ is about
$0.67 \times 10^{11}$, which is $6.7 \times 10^{10}$: about 67 billion
worlds. The cell prints a 4 with 21 zeros, and then about
66,666,666,667.

This is a "suppose". Nobody knows the real chance. The chain shows what
one guess would mean. With a chance of one in $4 \times 10^{21}$, the
chain ends at exactly 1: a number times its partner is 1.

</details>

## 7. What went differently here?

Here is a worked line, with one step that went differently.

> □² + 9 = 25. Take the side of each part: side(□²) is □, side(9) is
> 3, and side(25) is 5. So □ + 3 = 5, and the box holds 2.

Put 2 in the box. Does □² + 9 make 25? What went differently?

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *Solving equations: keeping it level* and
   *Undoing a square: square roots, the side of a square*.
2. Put 2 in the box: 2 × 2 + 9. Is it 25?
3. Undo the steps in the opposite order. Which step built the left
   side last: squaring the box, or adding 9?

**Think about:** is side(16 + 9) the same as side(16) + side(9)?

**Try this next:** □² + 16 = 25.

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one answer</summary>

With 2 in the box, 2 × 2 + 9 is 13, not 25. The worked line took the
side of each part on its own. But the side of a sum is not the sum of
the sides: side(25) is 5, and side(16) + side(9) is 4 + 3, which is 7.

Keeping it level works. Adding 9 was the last step, so undo it first:
take 9 from both sides, and □² = 16. Then the box holds side(16), which
is 4. Check: 4 × 4 + 9 is 16 + 9, which is 25. Here is one answer.
Yours may be different and work too.

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *Multiplying fractions: a fraction of a
   fraction* and *Undoing a square: square roots, the side of a
   square*.
2. Count the small squares in the big square, and in the block. Then
   count the small squares along one side of each.
3. side(…) asks what, times itself, makes this. To multiply two
   fractions, multiply the tops, and multiply the bottoms.

**Think about:** a fraction of a fraction cut a square one way, and
then the other way. Here the two cuts are the same.

**Try this next:** side(1/100).

</details>

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>

The big square has 16 small squares, and the block has 9. So the block
is $\frac{9}{16}$ of the square. Along one side, the square has 4 small
squares, and the block has 3. So the block's side is $\frac{3}{4}$ of
the square's side.

side($\frac{9}{16}$) is $\frac{3}{4}$, because
$\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{9}{16}$. And $\frac{3}{4}$ is
$\frac{12}{16}$, which is bigger than $\frac{9}{16}$. For a fraction
between 0 and 1, the side is bigger than the fraction. side(1/100) is
1/10.

</details>

## 17. A stretch: a fraction in the box

Here is a box in an exponent:

$$4^\square = 8$$

```question
id: a-stretch-a-fraction-in-the-box-1
type: fill-in-the-blank

4 is 2 to the power
{2|4|1/2}.

8 is 2 to the power
{3|8|4}.

So 4 to the power □ is 2 to the power 2 × □. Both sides are powers of
2, so the exponents balance: 2 × □ = 3. The box holds
{3/2|1|6}.
```

<details class="dl-hint"><summary>stuck? here are some steps</summary>

1. This uses the ideas from *A power of a power: stacks of stacks*,
   *Solving equations: keeping it level* and *Stretching the halfway
   steps: fractional exponents, the top and the bottom*.
2. Write 4 and 8 as powers of 2.
3. $\left(2^2\right)$ to the power □ is □ boxes of two 2s, so multiply

<details class="dl-answer"><summary>one way through it</summary>
